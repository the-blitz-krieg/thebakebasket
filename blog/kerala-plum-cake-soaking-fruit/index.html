Why Kerala Plum Cake Fruit Soaks for Weeks

PBy Priya ShellPublished 26 November 20263 min read

Dried fruit for a Kerala plum cake is soaked for weeks (sometimes months) so it plumps up, softens, and absorbs flavour, which keeps the finished cake moist and lets the fruit and spice blend into one deep taste. The soaking liquid is traditionally rum or brandy, which also helps the cake keep; juice works for an alcohol-free cake that's eaten sooner. The very dark colour comes from caramelised sugar, not from chocolate.

What goes into the jar

Black raisins, golden raisins, chopped dates, dried figs or apricots, candied orange peel, tutti-frutti, glazed cherries, cashews. Every family has its own mix and its own strong opinions. Some swear by adding ginger preserve; others insist on prunes.

The fruit is chopped, layered into a clean glass jar, and covered with the soaking liquid. Then it's sealed and left somewhere cool and dark, and stirred or shaken now and then.

What the soaking does

It softens the fruit. Dried fruit is tough and chewy. Weeks of soaking turn it plump and almost jammy.

It stops the fruit drying the cake. Dry fruit pulls moisture out of the batter as the cake bakes. Fruit that's already full of liquid gives moisture back instead, which is why a good plum cake stays moist for weeks.

It blends the flavours. Over time, the fruits taste less like separate ingredients and more like one thing, rich and dark and slightly boozy.

It helps the cake keep. Alcohol is a preservative. A rum-soaked plum cake, well wrapped, keeps far longer than most cakes.

Alcohol or not?

Rum and brandy are the traditional choices in Kerala, and they give a deeper, warmer flavour. The alcohol largely bakes off from the cake itself, but some remains, especially if the cake is "fed" with a little more after baking.

For families who don't drink, or who are serving children, the fruit can be soaked in orange juice, grape juice or strong black tea. The cake tastes lighter and fruitier, and it doesn't keep as long, so it's best within a week or two.

The caramel

Kerala plum cake is darker than most fruit cakes, and the secret isn't cocoa. It's caramel: sugar cooked in a pan until it turns very dark brown, almost burnt, then thinned with hot water. This syrup goes into the batter and gives the cake its deep colour and a faint bitterness that balances all the sweet fruit.

Getting the caramel right is the difference between a good plum cake and a great one. Too pale, and the cake looks anaemic. Too dark, and it tastes burnt. It's done by eye and smell.

The spices

Cinnamon, clove, nutmeg, and sometimes a little dry ginger and cardamom. Ground fresh if possible. When the cake is baking, the whole house smells like December.

How long does it keep?

Plum cake Keeps for
Rum or brandy soaked, well wrapped, cool place 3 to 4 weeks, sometimes longer
Rum or brandy soaked, in the fridge Up to 2 months
Alcohol-free (juice soaked) About 1 to 2 weeks in a cool place, longer in the fridge

Many people think a plum cake tastes best a few days after baking, once the flavours have settled. Wrap it in butter paper and then in foil or a cloth, and keep it in a tin.

Why is it called "plum" cake when there are no plums?

In old English, "plum" was used for raisins and dried fruit in general. That's why there's a plum pudding with no plums in it. The name came to India with the British, and stuck.

FAQs

Questions people ask

Why do you soak fruit for plum cake?

Soaking plumps and softens the dried fruit, keeps the cake moist by stopping the fruit pulling moisture from the batter, blends the flavours, and, if alcohol is used, helps the cake keep longer.

How long should fruit be soaked for plum cake?

Many Kerala bakers soak fruit for several weeks, and some for months. Even a few days of soaking improves the cake, but longer soaking gives a deeper flavour.

Can plum cake be made without alcohol?

Yes. Fruit can be soaked in orange juice, grape juice or tea instead. Alcohol-free plum cake tastes lighter and should be eaten within about one to two weeks.
