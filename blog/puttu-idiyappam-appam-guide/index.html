Puttu, Idiyappam and Appam: A Guide to Kerala's Steamed Breakfasts

BBy Beth MathewPublished 26 November 20263 min read

Puttu is steamed rice flour layered with grated coconut in a tall cylinder. Idiyappam (string hoppers, called sevai in parts of Tamil Nadu) is rice dough pressed into fine noodles and steamed into soft nests. Appam is a fermented rice and coconut batter cooked in a curved pan into a lacy pancake with a soft middle. All three are rice-based, and all three are breakfast staples in Kerala and in many Tamil homes. You'll find them on our traditional steamed foods page.

Puttu

How it's made. Rice flour is moistened with salted water until it's damp and crumbly, like wet sand, but not a dough. It's layered into a puttu kutti (a steaming cylinder) with grated coconut between the layers, and steamed for a few minutes. It slides out as a log striped white with coconut.

Texture. Soft, crumbly, light. It falls apart when you press it with your fingers, which is exactly how you're meant to eat it.

Eat it with. Kadala curry (black chickpea curry) is the classic. Also banana and sugar, ripe pappadam crushed over it, or fish curry. In some homes, a spoonful of ghee and sugar for children.

Variations. Ragi puttu, wheat puttu, corn puttu, and chiratta puttu, steamed in a coconut shell.

Idiyappam

How it's made. Rice flour is cooked with hot water into a soft dough, then pressed through a mould (a sevai nazhi) into thin noodles onto steaming plates, in small nests. Steamed for about ten minutes.

Texture. Soft, delicate, light. Each strand separate, the whole nest gently springy.

Eat it with. Kerala stew, egg curry, chicken curry, kurma. Sweet coconut milk with sugar and cardamom. Grated coconut and sugar for a simple breakfast.

In Tamil Nadu. It's often called sevai and turned into lemon sevai, coconut sevai or tamarind sevai, tossed with tempering.

Appam

How it's made. Rice is ground with coconut and fermented with yeast, or traditionally with toddy, overnight. The batter is poured into a hot appachatti (a small, deep, curved pan) and swirled, so it spreads thin up the sides and pools in the centre. Covered and cooked without flipping.

Texture. Crisp, lacy, golden edges; thick, soft, spongy centre. The contrast is the whole point.

Eat it with. Stew, above all. Also egg roast, chicken curry, or coconut milk with sugar.

Its relatives. Vattayappam is a steamed, sweet, thick cake from a similar batter; we explain it in what is vattayappam. Kallappam is a flatter, toddy-fermented version.

Idli, for completeness

The Tamil classic needs no introduction in Chennai, but it fits here. Fermented rice and urad dal batter, steamed in moulds. Kanchipuram idli adds pepper, cumin, ginger and ghee. Rava idli uses semolina and curd and needs no fermentation. Millet idlis are increasingly popular for everyday breakfasts.

Ordering them in Chennai

Appam is best eaten within minutes of leaving the pan, so it's the hardest to deliver well. Puttu and idiyappam travel well if they're steamed close to delivery time and kept covered; a quick re-steam at home brings them back to fresh. Order them for a Sunday breakfast, a family visit or a lunch for someone recovering at home; they're gentle and easy to digest.

FAQs

Questions people ask

What is the difference between puttu and idiyappam?

Puttu is crumbly steamed rice flour layered with coconut in a cylinder, while idiyappam is rice dough pressed into fine noodles and steamed into soft nests. Both are rice-based breakfasts from Kerala and Tamil Nadu.

Is idiyappam the same as sevai?

Yes, they're essentially the same: steamed rice noodles. In Tamil Nadu, sevai is often tossed with lemon, coconut or tamarind seasoning after steaming.

Which is healthier, puttu or appam?

Both are made from rice and coconut. Puttu is steamed with no oil, while appam uses a little oil in the pan and is fermented. Ragi or wheat puttu adds extra fibre.
