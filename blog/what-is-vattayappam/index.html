What Is Vattayappam? Kerala's Soft Steamed Cake for Christmas and Easter

PBy Priya ShellPublished 26 November 20263 min read

Vattayappam (also spelt vattappam) is a soft, spongy, steamed rice cake from Kerala, made from a fermented batter of rice and coconut, sweetened with sugar and scented with cardamom, often topped with cashews and raisins. It's traditionally made for Christmas and Easter in Kerala Christian homes, and it tastes like a cross between a fluffy idli and a gentle sponge cake. You can order it fresh from our traditional steamed foods page.

What it tastes like

If you've never had vattayappam, imagine tearing a piece off a warm, white, round cake. It's springy, almost bouncy, with a fine honeycomb of tiny holes. It smells of cardamom and coconut, faintly of fermentation, like a good appam. It isn't too sweet. It's the kind of thing you eat one wedge of, then come back for a second while pretending to be on your way somewhere else.

On Christmas morning, after the midnight mass, it's often eaten with a spicy meat curry, the sweet and spicy together. At Easter, it sits beside the lamb stew. And on an ordinary afternoon, it's lovely on its own with a cup of tea.

Why it's fermented

The softness comes from fermentation. Rice is soaked and ground with coconut and a little cooked rice or rice flour paste, then left to ferment with yeast, or traditionally with toddy (kallu), the fermented palm sap that once gave Kerala's appams their particular flavour and lift. These days most homes use yeast.

During fermentation, the yeast feeds on the sugars in the batter and produces gas, which forms all those tiny bubbles. When the batter is steamed, the bubbles expand and set, and you get that light, springy crumb. Rush the ferment, and the vattayappam is flat and heavy. Over-ferment, and it's sour.

How it's made

  1. Soak raw rice for several hours.
  2. Grind it with freshly grated coconut into a smooth batter, adding a small amount of cooked rice for softness.
  3. Ferment with yeast and sugar for several hours, or overnight, depending on the weather. In Chennai's warmth, it ferments fast. In the cooler months, it takes longer.
  4. Flavour with crushed cardamom and sugar.
  5. Pour into a greased round plate and top with cashews and raisins, often fried lightly in ghee.
  6. Steam for about 20 to 25 minutes until it's risen, set and springs back when pressed.
  7. Cool a little before cutting, so the wedges come out clean.

Vattayappam, appam and kinnathappam

They're relatives. Appam (palappam) uses a similar fermented rice and coconut batter, but it's cooked in a curved pan to make a lacy, crisp-edged pancake with a soft centre. Vattayappam is the same family, steamed thick into a cake. Kinnathappam, from Malabar, is a different steamed sweet, smoother and denser, made with rice flour and coconut milk, set and sliced.

Christmas and Easter

In many Kerala Christian homes, Christmas preparations include the plum cake, the wine (or grape juice) set to ferment weeks earlier, the meat curry, and vattayappam made on Christmas Eve or early on Christmas morning. At Easter, after the long Lenten fast, vattayappam returns to the table. For families living in Chennai, away from the tharavad and the grandmothers who made it, it's often the dish that's most missed.

That's the reason we make it. Our Christmas plum cake sits beside it on the festive menu.

Keeping it

Vattayappam is best on the day it's steamed. It keeps in a covered container at room temperature for a day in cool weather, and in the fridge for two. Steam slices for a few minutes to soften them again.

FAQs

Questions people ask

What is vattayappam made of?

Vattayappam is made from rice and coconut ground into a batter, fermented with yeast (traditionally toddy), sweetened with sugar and flavoured with cardamom, then steamed. It's often topped with cashews and raisins.

When is vattayappam eaten?

Vattayappam is traditionally made for Christmas and Easter in Kerala Christian homes, but many families enjoy it year-round with tea or with curries.

What's the difference between appam and vattayappam?

Both use a fermented rice and coconut batter. Appam is cooked in a curved pan into a thin, lacy pancake, while vattayappam is steamed into a thick, soft, sweet cake.
