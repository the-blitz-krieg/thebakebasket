How to Cut a Round Cake Neatly (and Get More Slices)

PBy Priya ShellPublished 26 November 20263 min read

Use a long, thin knife, dip it in hot water and wipe it dry before every cut, and cut straight down without sawing. For a cake bigger than 20 cm, don't cut wedges from the centre; cut a ring about 5 cm in from the edge, slice that ring into pieces, then repeat with the inner circle. You'll get neater slices and far more of them.

I've watched beautiful cakes destroyed in thirty seconds by a blunt kitchen knife and good intentions. It's always someone's uncle, and he always says he knows what he's doing. Here's what actually works.

What you need

  • A long, thin, sharp knife. A bread knife is not ideal for cream cakes; it tears. A long chef's knife or a cake knife is better.
  • A tall jug of hot water.
  • A clean kitchen towel.
  • A cake server or a flat spatula.
  • Plates within reach, not across the room.

Chill it first (a little)

A cake straight from a warm room is soft and smears. A cake straight from the fridge is firm but the flavour is muted. The sweet spot is about 15 to 20 minutes out of the fridge for cream and buttercream cakes: firm enough to cut cleanly, soft enough to taste.

The warm-blade trick

Dip the knife in hot water for a few seconds, wipe it dry with the towel, and cut. The warm blade slides through frosting instead of dragging it. Do this before every single cut. Yes, every one. It's the single biggest difference between neat slices and a smeared mess.

Cut straight down in one smooth motion, pressing gently, then pull the knife out towards you rather than lifting it back up through the slice.

Small cakes (up to about 20 cm): wedges

For a 500 g to 1 kg round cake, wedges are fine.

  1. Cut the cake in half.
  2. Cut each half in half again, so you have quarters.
  3. Cut each quarter into two or three wedges, depending on how many you need.

A 1 kg round cut this way gives 8 to 12 slices.

Bigger cakes: the ring method

Wedges from a big cake are huge at the outside and nearly nothing at the tip. The ring method fixes that.

  1. Cut a circle about 5 cm in from the outer edge, all the way around.
  2. Cut that outer ring into slices about 2.5 to 3 cm wide.
  3. Cut another circle 5 cm further in.
  4. Slice that ring the same way.
  5. Keep going until the small centre is left, then cut it into wedges.

A 2 kg cake cut with this method easily serves 25 neat pieces.

Tiered cakes

  1. Remove the top tier first (and any toppers, dowels and supports, which are not edible).
  2. Cut the bottom tier with the ring method.
  3. Cut the top tier separately, or keep it for the family.

Some families keep the top tier of a wedding or anniversary cake for later. If you plan to, tell your baker, and we'll tell you how long that particular cake will keep.

Fondant cakes

Fondant resists the knife. Score through the fondant layer first with the tip of a warm knife, then cut down through the cake. Many guests will peel the fondant off anyway, so don't worry if the sides aren't perfect.

Cheesecakes and brownies

Cheesecakes love the warm-blade trick more than anything else. Brownies cut best when they're cool and slightly firm; cutting warm brownies tastes wonderful and looks terrible.

Serving

Lay each slice on its side on the plate, not upright, so it doesn't topple. And give the birthday person the first slice, the one with their name on it. It's tradition for a reason.

If you'd like a cake that's easier to cut, ask for a buttercream or ganache finish over fresh cream; they hold clean edges better. More on that in our cakes page.

FAQs

Questions people ask

How do you cut a cake without it crumbling?

Chill the cake for a short while, use a long thin knife dipped in hot water and wiped dry before each cut, and cut straight down without sawing.

How many slices do you get from a 1 kg cake?

A 1 kg round cake gives 8 to 12 party slices when cut into wedges, and a few more with the ring method.

What's the best way to cut a big round cake?

Cut a circle about 5 cm in from the edge, slice that ring into even pieces, then repeat with the next circle in. It gives neater, more even slices than cutting wedges from the centre.
