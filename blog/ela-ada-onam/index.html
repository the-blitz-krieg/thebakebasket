Ela Ada: The Banana-Leaf Sweet That Tastes Like Onam

BBy Beth MathewPublished 26 November 20263 min read

Ela ada (also written elayada or ila ada) is a Kerala sweet made by spreading a thin layer of rice-flour dough on a piece of banana leaf, filling it with coconut and jaggery, folding it closed and steaming it. "Ela" means leaf and "ada" is the rice parcel. The leaf gives it a faint, green, almost smoky fragrance that nothing else can. It's made all year as a tea-time snack, and with special affection at Onam.

If you're in Chennai and missing it, our traditional steamed foods include ela ada made the old way.

The moment you open it

That's what people remember. You unfold the leaf, still warm, and the steam carries the smell of jaggery, coconut and wilted banana leaf all at once. The ada itself is soft and slightly sticky, almost translucent at the edges, and the leaf leaves a faint imprint of its veins on the rice. The filling has melted into a dark, sweet, coconut-heavy layer that's best eaten with your fingers, carefully, because it's hot.

What goes into it

The dough. Rice flour (traditionally, roasted rice flour) mixed with hot water and a pinch of salt into a soft, spreadable dough. Some homes add a little coconut oil or ghee.

The filling. Freshly grated coconut cooked with jaggery until thick and glossy, flavoured with cardamom. Some families add ripe banana, especially the Kerala nendran banana. Others add jackfruit when it's in season, which makes chakka ada, one of the most loved variations.

The leaf. Fresh banana leaf, cut into rectangles and softened by passing it briefly over a flame or dipping it in hot water, so it folds without tearing.

How it's made

  1. Wipe the banana leaf pieces clean and soften them.
  2. Spread a thin layer of dough on one half of the leaf with wet fingers.
  3. Spoon the filling along the centre.
  4. Fold the leaf over, press the edges gently to seal the dough.
  5. Steam for about 10 to 15 minutes, until the dough turns slightly translucent.
  6. Let it rest for a few minutes before unwrapping. The ada sets a little as it cools.

The hard part is the thinness. Too thick and it's stodgy. Too thin and it tears. There's a reason grandmothers made it look easy.

Ela ada and Onam

Onam is Kerala's harvest festival, and the sadhya, the great vegetarian feast on a banana leaf, is its centre. Ela ada isn't usually part of the sadhya itself, but it's one of the sweets that appears around the festival, made in homes in the days before and after, offered to visitors with tea. Ada pradhaman, the payasam made with rice ada, is the sadhya's star dessert, and it shares a name and a family resemblance.

For Malayali families in Chennai, Onam often means a sadhya ordered from a caterer, a pookalam made in a small flat entrance, and a longing for the snacks that a grandmother would have made without being asked. That's where ela ada comes in.

Variations worth knowing

  • Chakka ada: with jackfruit, during jackfruit season
  • Banana ada: with ripe nendran banana mashed into the filling
  • Wheat ada: using wheat flour instead of rice, a little more chewy
  • Ari ada: the plain rice version, often for ada pradhaman

Keeping it

Ela ada is best eaten warm, on the day. In the fridge it keeps for a day, wrapped in its leaf. Steam it again for five minutes to bring back the softness.

Questions people ask

What is ela ada?

Ela ada is a Kerala snack made by spreading rice-flour dough on banana leaf, filling it with coconut and jaggery, folding it and steaming it. The leaf gives it a distinctive fragrance.

Is ela ada healthy?

Ela ada is steamed, not fried, and made with rice, coconut and jaggery, which makes it a lighter sweet than many fried snacks. It still contains sugar from the jaggery, so it's a treat.

Can I order ela ada in Chennai?
