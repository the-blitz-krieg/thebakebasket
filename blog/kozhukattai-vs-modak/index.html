Kozhukattai or Modak? How the Two Dumplings Differ

PBy Priya ShellPublished 26 November 20263 min read

Kozhukattai and modak are close cousins: both are rice-flour dumplings filled with coconut and jaggery and steamed for Lord Ganesha. The differences are mostly in shape and region. Maharashtrian ukadiche modak is usually pleated into a tall, pointed teardrop and often richer with ghee. Tamil kozhukattai comes in more shapes, half-moons, pouches and hand-pressed pidi, and has savoury versions too.

People sometimes ask me this when they order for Vinayagar Chathurthi, often in families where one side is Tamil and the other grew up in Pune or Mumbai. The honest answer is that both are right. We make both on our traditional steamed foods page.

Shape

This is the difference you notice first.

Modak is shaped into a dome that rises to a point, with many fine pleats running up the sides, sometimes as many as eleven or more. A good modak-maker's pleats are thin and even. In many Maharashtrian homes, the number and neatness of the pleats are a matter of quiet pride.

Kozhukattai has more variety. Some homes pleat it into a pouch similar to modak. Many fold it into a half-moon and crimp the edge. Pidi kozhukattai is simply squeezed in the fist. There's less fuss about the exact shape, and more about the filling and the softness of the shell.

Filling

Both share the classic: grated coconut and jaggery, flavoured with cardamom.

Modak fillings are often enriched with ghee, sometimes nutmeg, poppy seeds or dry fruits. There are also fried modak (talniche modak) with a wheat-flour shell, and modern versions with chocolate, mawa and even ice cream.

Kozhukattai has its sweet versions (coconut and jaggery, or sesame and jaggery) and a distinct savoury one, ulundu kozhukattai, filled with spiced, steamed urad dal. That savoury branch is one of the biggest differences between the two traditions.

Dough

Both use rice flour cooked with hot water into a pliable dough. Maharashtrian recipes often add a little ghee or milk to the dough for a softer, richer shell. Tamil recipes typically use water with a few drops of oil. The dough itself is otherwise very similar; much of the difference is technique and family habit.

Festival and region

Ganesh Chaturthi in Maharashtra is a big public festival, and modak is the signature food, with households and sweet shops making hundreds. In Tamil Nadu, Vinayagar Chathurthi is quieter and more home-centred: a small clay idol, a pooja at home, and kozhukattai as the offering. Karnataka has its own version too, often called kadubu.

Which should you make, or order?

Make the one your family makes. If your family is both, make both. Vinayagar is not known to be fussy.

If you're ordering, tell us the shape your family is used to. A Maharashtrian grandparent will notice if the modak doesn't have its point, and a Tamil grandmother will notice if there's no savoury kozhukattai on the plate.

FAQs

Questions people ask

Are modak and kozhukattai the same?

They're very similar: both are steamed rice-flour dumplings with a coconut and jaggery filling offered to Ganesha. Modak is the Maharashtrian version, usually pleated into a pointed shape, while kozhukattai is the Tamil version, made in several shapes and also in savoury forms.

Which is offered for Vinayagar Chathurthi in Tamil Nadu?

In Tamil Nadu, kozhukattai is the traditional offering for Vinayagar Chathurthi, usually both sweet and savoury kinds.

Can I order both modak and kozhukattai in Chennai?

Yes. The Bake Basket makes both for Vinayagar Chathurthi. Order a few days ahead and mention the shape and fillings your family prefers.
