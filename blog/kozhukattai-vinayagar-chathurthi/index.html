Kozhukattai for Vinayagar Chathurthi: The Kinds, the Meaning, the Making

PBy Priya ShellPublished 26 November 20263 min read

Kozhukattai is the steamed rice dumpling made in Tamil homes for Vinayagar Chathurthi, offered to Lord Vinayagar (Ganesha) before the family eats. The best-known kind is sweet, with a coconut and jaggery filling called poornam, but there are also savoury kozhukattai filled with spiced urad dal and pidi kozhukattai pressed by hand. If you'd like to order fresh ones for the festival, our traditional steamed foods page has the details. Book early; the steamer is busy that week.

Why kozhukattai for Vinayagar?

Vinayagar is often described in stories as being especially fond of modak and kozhukattai. In many tellings, the sweet's shape, a soft outside holding something precious within, is taken as a symbol: an ordinary covering around sweetness, the way wisdom is hidden inside the everyday. Families have their own versions of the meaning. What everyone agrees on is that it's the food of the day, and that the first ones are always for him.

The kinds

Sweet kozhukattai (poornam kozhukattai). The classic. A thin, soft shell of cooked rice-flour dough around a filling of freshly grated coconut, jaggery and cardamom, often with a little ghee. Shaped like a pleated pouch or a half-moon.

Ellu kozhukattai. Filled with roasted sesame seeds and jaggery. Nutty, a little drier, very traditional in some homes.

Ulundu kozhukattai (savoury). Filled with a steamed, spiced urad dal mixture, tempered with mustard seeds, curry leaves and green chilli. For the people at the table who find the sweet version too much after the third one.

Pidi kozhukattai. "Pidi" means a fistful. The dough is mixed with jaggery and coconut, or with a savoury tempering, and squeezed by hand into a shape that carries the marks of your fingers. No filling, no pleats. Rustic, quick, delicious.

Modak. The Maharashtrian cousin, often with a pointed top and many pleats. More on how they differ in kozhukattai or modak.

How it's made, the traditional way

  1. Rice. Traditionally, raw rice is soaked, dried, ground into fine flour and lightly roasted. Many homes now use store-bought rice flour; the soaked-and-ground version gives a softer, smoother shell.
  2. The dough. Water is boiled with a pinch of salt and a few drops of oil, then the flour is stirred in and cooked into a smooth dough. It's kneaded while warm, which is the part that tests your palms.
  3. The poornam. Grated coconut and jaggery are cooked together until the mixture thickens and comes together, then finished with cardamom.
  4. Shaping. A small ball of dough is pressed into a thin cup, filled, and closed with pleats or folded into a half-moon. Thin is the goal. A thick shell is the most common mistake.
  5. Steaming. Ten to twelve minutes in a steamer, until the outside turns faintly glossy and translucent.

Eating and keeping

Kozhukattai is best warm, within a few hours of steaming. They keep in the fridge for a day; steam them again for two or three minutes before eating and they soften right back. Don't microwave them uncovered, or the shell turns rubbery.

FAQs

Questions people ask

Why is kozhukattai made on Vinayagar Chathurthi?

Kozhukattai is traditionally considered Lord Vinayagar's favourite food. Families steam it on the morning of the festival and offer it to him before eating it themselves.

What are the different types of kozhukattai?

The main kinds are sweet kozhukattai with coconut and jaggery, ellu kozhukattai with sesame, savoury ulundu kozhukattai with spiced urad dal, and pidi kozhukattai pressed by hand.

How long does kozhukattai stay fresh?

Kozhukattai is best eaten the same day. It keeps in the fridge for about a day and should be re-steamed for a few minutes before eating.
