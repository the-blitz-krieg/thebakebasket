Choosing a Cake When Someone in the Family Has Diabetes

BBy Beth MathewPublished 26 November 20263 min read

There's no cake that's simply "safe" for diabetics, but there are much better choices. Pick a smaller cake made with less sugar, more fibre (whole wheat, millets, nuts) and a lighter frosting; serve a thin slice after a meal rather than on its own; and ask your baker plainly what's in it. Above all, follow the advice your family member's doctor has given, because diabetes is managed differently for different people.

I'm not a doctor, and this isn't medical advice. It's what we've learned from many families who've asked us the same question: how do we celebrate Appa's 70th without him sitting out the cake?

First, the words on the menu

"Sugar-free" can mean many things. Sometimes it means a sugar substitute like a sugar alcohol or stevia blend has been used instead of sugar. Those can help, but the cake still contains flour, which becomes glucose in the body, and some substitutes affect blood sugar more than others.

"No added sugar" or "no refined sugar" often means the cake is sweetened with jaggery, dates, honey or fruit. These are still sugars.

"Reduced sugar" means less sugar than the usual recipe. Less, not none.

We go through all three in detail in sugar-free, no added sugar, reduced sugar.

What tends to be a better choice

Smaller, richer cakes. A thin slice of something satisfying beats a big slice of something light. A dense ragi brownie or a nutty date and walnut loaf is eaten slowly.

More fibre. Whole wheat, ragi, oats and nuts slow down how quickly the cake is digested compared with a pure maida sponge.

Less frosting. The frosting is often the most sugar-dense part. A simple cake with a thin layer, or a loaf cake with no frosting, is a better pick than a cream-loaded celebration cake.

Reduced sugar overall. Ask the baker to cut sugar in the sponge, the syrup and the frosting.

Fresh fruit on top instead of syrupy fillings.

What tends to be a worse choice

  • Large slices of soft maida sponge with lots of cream or buttercream
  • Syrup-soaked cakes like rasmalai and tres leches style cakes
  • Fondant, which is almost pure sugar
  • Cakes eaten on an empty stomach, mid-afternoon
  • Anything labelled "diabetic-friendly" without telling you exactly what's in it

Practical ideas for the day

Two cakes. A smaller, regular celebration cake for the cutting and photos, plus a reduced-sugar millet bake or loaf for the people watching sugar. Nobody feels singled out.

A small cake for the birthday person. If it's Appa's birthday, give him his own bento-sized reduced-sugar cake with his name on it. It's his moment, not a compromise.

Cut thinly. Offer a sliver. Many people just want the taste and the ritual.

Time it after the meal. Cake after a balanced dinner affects blood sugar less dramatically than cake on its own.

Talk to the doctor. Some people with well-managed diabetes can enjoy a small piece of regular cake; others are advised strictly. Their own doctor knows.

What to ask your baker

  1. What sweetener is in the sponge, the filling and the frosting?
  2. How much less sugar than your normal recipe?
  3. What flour is used?
  4. Can the frosting be lighter, or can we skip it?
  5. If you use a sugar substitute, which one?

An honest baker will answer clearly, and won't promise you anything they can't stand behind. At The Bake Basket, the healthy bakes are labelled with exactly what's in them, and nothing is ever called diabetic-safe.

FAQs

Questions people ask

Can diabetics eat birthday cake?

Many people with diabetes can enjoy a small portion of cake occasionally, especially after a meal, but it depends on their individual condition and their doctor's advice. Choose smaller portions, less frosting and reduced-sugar, higher-fibre recipes.

Is sugar-free cake safe for diabetics?

Not automatically. Sugar-free cakes still contain flour, which raises blood glucose, and some sweeteners affect blood sugar too. Treat sugar-free cake as a better option, not a free one.

What is the best cake for a diabetic person?

A small portion of a reduced-sugar cake made with whole grains, millets or nuts, with little or no frosting, is usually a better choice than a regular cream cake.
