Our story

Love, baked to share

Some children follow their mothers to the market. Priya followed the smell of the oven.

As a little girl in Chennai she was the one on tiptoe at the kitchen counter, flour on her elbows, asking if she could crack the egg this time. Long before she knew what a talent was, she knew what happened to people's faces when something warm came out of the oven. They softened. They leaned in. They stayed a little longer than they had planned to.

It took her years to understand that this wasn't a hobby she had picked up along the way. It felt more like something she had been trusted with. And the thing about a gift is that it is meant to be given away.

It starts before the kitchen

Most bakers start with a recipe. Priya starts with a person.

When she walks through the market she isn't really shopping. She's picturing faces. The speckled bananas become a loaf for a grandmother who likes something soft with her evening coffee. The dark chocolate becomes brownies a teenager will hide from his brother. The jaggery and grated coconut become kozhukattai for a family that misses how festival mornings used to taste.

By the time she's home, the bakes already have names. The names of the people they're for.

Knitting flavours together

Nothing in her kitchen is rushed and nothing is thrown together. Priya says she knits flavours: a little cardamom worked into a sponge, a pinch of sea salt over caramel, strong filter coffee folded into a frosting, until they hold together like something well made.

She measures carefully. She also measures the way mothers do, with her eyes, with her memory, and with one question that every batch has to pass: would I give this to my own children? If the answer is anything less than yes, it doesn't leave her kitchen.

Something from nothing

There's a small magic in baking that has never worn off for her. Flour, butter, sugar, a few eggs. Ordinary things from an ordinary shelf. Then hands, heat and patience, and suddenly there's a birthday cake that makes a six-year-old gasp, or a loaf that fills a whole flat with the smell of home.

She doesn't use ready mixes. She doesn't bake ahead and wait for someone to buy. Your order starts when you place it, so what reaches you is as fresh as what she'd put on her own table.

Food says what words can't

She has watched it happen again and again. A box of cupcakes that said sorry better than any message could. A tray of soft idiyappam carried to a friend just home from hospital. An anniversary cake that made a couple married forty years laugh like they'd only just met.

Food reaches the places words don't. It looks after people. It brings them back to each other. Without a speech, it says: you matter to me.

Maybe that's why people who taste her baking rarely ask "how much?" first. They ask, "when can I order again?"

Why a basket

In every Indian home there's a basket that never stays home. It goes to the temple and the church, to the hospital, to the new baby, to the neighbour on festival morning. A basket is never for yourself. It's how we carry love to someone else.

That's The Bake Basket. Priya bakes it. You carry it. And somewhere across the city, someone you love takes the first bite and goes quiet for a moment.

That moment is what she bakes for.

What I promise you

I won't use a ready mix. I won't bake your order before you've placed it. And I won't send you anything I wouldn't serve at my own table.

FAQs

Questions people ask

Who runs The Bake Basket?

The Bake Basket is run by Priya Shell, a mother and home baker in Chennai who bakes every order herself.

What makes The Bake Basket different from a regular bakery?

Every order is baked only after it's placed, by one person, with ingredients she chooses herself and no ready mixes. The price is confirmed in writing before she starts.

Why is it called The Bake Basket?

Because in every Indian home a basket is how we carry food to someone else: to the temple, the hospital, the new baby, the neighbour on festival morning. Priya bakes it, you carry it.

Does Priya bake traditional South Indian foods as well as cakes?

Yes. Alongside cakes and breads she makes steamed foods from Tamil and Kerala kitchens, including kozhukattai, vattayappam, ela ada, puttu and idiyappam.
